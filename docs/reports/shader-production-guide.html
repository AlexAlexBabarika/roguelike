Make the affordable asset the intended asset

Keep all four directions. Change the production test. The question is not whether a beautiful concept can be imitated. It is whether a coarse model, repeatable materials and a reusable rig look convincing through the actual combat camera. Storm Fresco is the strongest surface-unification route; Woven Blight offers the strongest construction language for simplified bodies.

I interpret “wipe out the models” as having to produce the models and characters ourselves, quickly, with a Blender-first pipeline. If you meant destructible bodies instead, dismemberment is a separate asset and rigging problem. This is a proposal, not a selected style or an implemented renderer.

Four production recipes

Choose a tab for the model construction, proposed shader stack and failure case. These are qualitative assessments, not measured cost estimates.

Storm Fresco — best at unifying rough surfaces

Minimum character: a hooded head without facial detail, one broad coat volume, block gloves, tapered boots and a readable long weapon. Trees use a few large branch masses; clinic walls use broad planes and limited bevels. Do not require hand-painted detail on every object.

Conceals well
Inconsistent texture realism, missing skin detail, cheap surface construction and modest polygon density away from silhouettes.
Does not conceal
Wrong proportions, bad elbow bending or a weapon that disappears against the floor.
Build language
A single painted crescent grows thicker with amplification, then repeats once for an echo. This is an authored effect shape, not random brush noise.
Proposed shader stack and first failure test
  1. Matte base: shared material palette, high roughness and restrained direct specular. Start with ordinary lit materials so rain lamps and shadows remain coherent.
  2. Stable paint masks: broad, curated patches attached to the surface. Triplanar mapping is useful for static walls and rocks; use UVs or another deformation-stable mapping for skinned characters.
  3. Soft, limited lighting bands: try a small number of tonal families rather than a harsh binary cel split. Keep shadow and light attenuation; do not quantize away an approaching enemy.
  4. Separate water: a live reflection with broad ripples and controlled contrast, not the same matte shader applied everywhere.

Watch for: “painterly” noise becoming camouflage. Move the lamp around a coarse head: if bands crawl over bad normals or the brush pattern swims during a dodge, simplify the treatment and fix the source mesh.

Woven Blight — best at making simplified bodies intentional

Minimum character: a compressed-felt torso, hood or blank sack head, thick canvas limbs and rope cuffs covering the joints. Use one reusable skeleton beneath a small set of body parts. Enemies can be differently tensioned bundles; asymmetry must be deliberate and consistent, not a justification for arbitrary mesh errors.

Conceals well
Missing anatomy, crude hands, simple joints, limited textures and primitive-based construction—because the body is not supposed to be realistic flesh.
Does not conceal
Weightless movement, foot sliding, mesh clipping or a wind-up hidden by loose cloth.
Build language
A marked seam tightens, a knot adds an amplifier, then a loop snaps into a second strike. Reserve strong seam contrast for gameplay-relevant parts.
Proposed shader stack and first failure test
  1. Opaque cloth base: a shared coarse weave texture with subtle normal variation and matte lighting. No fur shells, per-fibre strands or whole-world cloth simulation.
  2. Real large seams: a few modeled seams and cuffs carry the silhouette; bake or texture the fine stitching. A surface shader cannot create meaningful seam thickness at the contour.
  3. Controlled wetness: darker damp cloth with slightly altered roughness. Keep major glossy highlights on oilcloth puddles rather than every character.
  4. Secondary movement only: shader sway on a hanging strip or nearby vegetation. Leave the body, weapon and attack footprint tied to the actual rig and gameplay.

Watch for: texture swimming on skinned limbs and cute doll proportions. Use deformation-stable mapping and adult-scale, heavy shapes. Test a complete strike before adding fibre detail.

Amber Archive — reduce the optical ambition, not the identity

Minimum character: an opaque charcoal body with one large, smooth specimen plate. Build the plate as a clean shell with an inset dark branching form or a depth illusion. Most trees and clinic walls stay matte; amber is a rare emphasis.

Conceals well
Unmodeled internal detail behind a stylized opaque plate; rough background assets when optical accents are sparse.
Exposes badly
Bad normals, intersections, jagged shell contours and weak interiors. Shine reveals the surface; transparency reveals what is underneath.
Build language
A branching inclusion fills a specimen plate; nested rings record an amplifier; a second bounded ring becomes the echo.
Proposed shader stack and first failure test
  1. Opaque first: a tinted shell, controlled specular and a shallow inset. A UV-based layered interior illusion is an option, not a verified cheap solution.
  2. No obligatory translucency: the preserved-looking form should read even when transparency and refraction are disabled.
  3. Limit overlap: test genuine transparency on one stationary specimen before applying it to combatants. Sorting, overdraw and screen-space refraction need independent validation.
  4. Spend model quality selectively: the silhouette and normals of the one visible specimen plate matter more than decorative props around it.

Watch for: the glossy plate making a coarse chest look worse, or repeated shells obscuring attack poses. This is the least forgiving choice if low-quality character models are the main constraint.

Cold Flash — accepts sparse assets, but not broken poses

Minimum character: a deliberately faceted coat, a simple mask, large hands and a recognizable weapon. Clinic props use clear low-detail proportions. Design the objects through the intended camera rather than building realistic objects and degrading them afterward.

Conceals well
Missing microdetail and restrained texture work. It makes low-detail construction acceptable rather than literally repairing it.
Exposes badly
Ugly silhouette, bad proportions and blocky bending under severe light. Low resolution can also erase a blade or an enemy’s raised arm.
Build language
Crisp local registration marks and bounded impacts, not global flashes, grain bursts or trailing historical positions.
Proposed shader stack and first failure test
  1. Modest internal resolution: render into a viewport, then upscale. Choose the resolution from actor and weapon readability; no preset resolution guarantees success.
  2. Restrained palette treatment: preserve silhouette separation and warning colours. Aggressive global quantization can collapse both into the same dark tone.
  3. Very subtle grain or ordered dithering: test whether it adds anything. It is optional, not the source of the art direction.
  4. Full-speed combat: no vertex wobble, slowed pose updates, input delay or screen flicker to simulate an old system.

Watch for: the world becoming a generic retro filter, or weather and tiny actors turning into the same pixels. Test reduced resolution with a moving blade, then introduce rain.

Recommendation: Woven Blight if characters are the hardest asset problem; Storm Fresco if material consistency and environmental asset throughput are harder. Keep Amber Archive as a serious candidate only with its opaque-shell variant. Cold Flash remains viable, but “low-res” alone is not a distinctive art direction.

Shader architecture: abstraction without hiding the fight

  1. Environment: shared material families, restrained contrast and stable surface marks. Fog limits background detail; it must not be the only thing concealing an enemy.
  2. Actors: calmer textures, clear limb separation and readable facing. Use a restrained actor-only rim or outline if needed; outlining every tree creates noise and costs extra drawing.
  3. Attack warnings: clear boundaries, timing and shape, not colour alone. Prefer local material stylization so warnings do not inherit grain or paint noise. If a global pass is added, define a real bypass or compositing strategy; saying “keep warnings clean” is not enough.
  4. UI: render after the world treatment. Damage numbers and effect explanations should not be pixelated or smeared to match the world.

Reflections deserve the same design rules: a puddle can reveal the underside of a coarse model and duplicate noisy effects. Use coherent live reflections with intentional ripple and contrast limits, not mirror-sharp fidelity everywhere. There is no need to promise ray tracing. Wetness can be darkening and selective highlights rather than a gloss multiplier on every surface.

Avoid as camouflage: heavy fog in the fighting zone, per-frame random texture noise, full-screen blur, aggressive outlines on every object, dithering that destroys telegraphs, and vertex deformation on an attack-bearing limb. These can hide defects by hiding information the player needs.

Godot implementation constraints verified against documentation
  • StandardMaterial3D already supports material controls and triplanar projection. Start with shared materials before custom full-screen infrastructure.
  • Spatial shaders support toon diffuse/specular modes and custom lighting. A custom light() function is not run when vertex lighting or forced vertex shading is enabled. Validate the target platform’s settings rather than assuming the desktop result carries over.
  • Screen-reading shaders do not capture transparent geometry in the ordinary 3D screen copy. Rain, water and amber cannot be assumed to appear in every screen-space effect. The normal/roughness texture is Forward+ only.
  • Compositor effects require Mobile or Forward+, not Compatibility. The existing graphics demo uses Compatibility. That does not prevent these styles: material shaders and an ordinary viewport-based final treatment are a different route.
  • World-space triplanar patterns can slide over moving objects. Object-local projection follows rigid objects, but does not automatically solve skinned deformation. Use a shared UV layout for characters if it gives a more stable result.
  • Extra outline geometry, texture samples, transparent layers and reflection viewports all have costs. No direction has a verified performance budget yet; the eventual target devices remain an open decision.

The useful next experiment: deliberately cheap assets

Do not make another polished illustration. If you approve a prototype, use one coarse player, one coarse enemy, a spruce, a clinic wall and a puddle. Start with a clean but simple baseline, not a damaged mesh engineered to fail.

  1. Lock the camera first. Decide target platforms, projection, actor screen size and the smallest intended viewport. Isometric framing reduces detail demands but does not remove silhouette or occlusion problems.
  2. Keep the asset recipe repeatable. Use headless Blender presets for dimensions, simple bevels, material slots and exports. Reuse a skeleton, simple collision proxies and a few animation clips. Generated meshes may still require repair or retopology; a shader does not make them automatically riggable.
  3. Compare two treatments first. Fresco and Woven share the same underlying rig and combat timings. Give each its necessary minimal construction changes—hood versus wrapped joints—and record those changes separately from shader changes.
  4. Test motion before weather. Idle, run, turn, dodge, wind-up and strike under a moving lamp. Then add wind, rain and live reflections individually. Finally enable a primer → amplifier → echo combination.
  5. Use pass/fail observations. Can a viewer identify facing, weapon direction and the damage boundary without relying on colour alone? Does the surface pattern stay attached? Does the style still work with decorative noise disabled? Do shader and reflection costs fit the chosen device budget?
The goal is not “bad models made invisible.” It is a style whose convincing models are cheap to make. A cloth cuff hiding an elbow, a hood replacing a face and a stable shared material often save more production effort than a sophisticated post-process.
