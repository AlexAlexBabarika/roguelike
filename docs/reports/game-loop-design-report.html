Combat, combinations and dread

The recommendation in one paragraph

Build a short-run action roguelite with deliberate combat and conditional, explosive synergies. A bounded rain-soaked forest leads into a deserted rural clinic. Learn enemy openings, assemble a four-slot build, use the environment to prepare an oversized strike, and choose whether the next reward is worth your remaining health. Let dread dominate the approach, precision dominate the fight, and power dominate the payoff. Do not demand all three emotions at maximum intensity at once.

Status: exploratory design report, not an approved game design or an implementation. Your requested ingredients are below; the particular loop, numbers, item names, enemies and progression rules are my proposals. The Blender/Godot graphics demo remains a separate rendering experiment. No game code was changed for this report.

The essential test: is one enemy enjoyable to fight without upgrades, and do a few upgrades change how you fight rather than only how fast its health disappears? If either answer is no, producing more art or procedural content is premature.

3. The identity: master the threat, then break its scale

The player should alternate between “I understand its attack” and “I discovered something outrageous.” The first comes from stable enemy rules and execution. The second comes from effects that reinforce one another. Horror lives partly in what happens before that understanding, and in the cost of committing to a setup while vulnerable.

  1. Deliberate, not sluggish. Positioning and attack commitments matter; movement and inputs should still feel immediate.
  2. Conditional power, not automatic power. An enormous result needs a target state, an opening, a location or a timing achievement.
  3. Discoverable interactions. Clearly explain rules. The surprise is recognizing a combination, not learning that an undocumented exception exists.
  4. The environment is part of the build. A puddle is useful or dangerous depending on your equipment and the enemy, rather than always a flat bonus.
  5. Fear without a writing dependency. A dry doorway, a body that has moved since your last glance, or a risky healing pause can carry tension without a conversation.

Emotional contract: this is horror-flavored action with moments of genuine dread, not constant helpless survival horror. A successful late-run build should sometimes turn a frightening enemy into a victim. Preventing that payoff to preserve fear would contradict your experimentation goal. If sustained helplessness becomes the main priority, this recommendation would need to change.

4. Choose a run structure, not three games at once

These are alternative architectures. The first is the recommendation; the other two show what we are deliberately not adding.

Recommended: a complete short run

Start in a quiet clinic vestibule, take a forest route, re-enter through the flooded service rooms, and finish with the guardian encounter in the clinic's rear wing. That is a proposed spatial loop, not a story requirement. For a later MVP, target roughly 20–30 minutes; the first playable slice should be much shorter. These are pacing hypotheses, not measured durations.

  1. Prepare: choose a weapon and one simple starting alteration; practice on a target. See the run objective without dialogue.
  2. Read the next route: choose between visible encounter/reward types, such as exposed wet clearing, narrow interior or optional elite cache.
  3. Approach: a short uneasy traversal establishes the threat before the arena. No upgrade shopping while an attack is imminent.
  4. Fight: learn or recall patterns, create a setup, execute it, survive recovery.
  5. Choose one of three rewards: extend the current engine, pivot it or take a useful defensive alternative. Comparison pauses the game after the encounter.
  6. Spend at a safe workbench: healing competes with an upgrade or reroll. Refit slots and inspect an example damage breakdown.
  7. Commit to the end: attempt an optional dangerous reward, then face the guardian with the assembled build.
  8. Finish or die: the build and run currency reset; learned rules and selected horizontal unlocks remain. A summary explains the strongest interaction and failure cause.

Why this fits: builds have a beginning, a middle and a payoff; difficulty can be paced around a known finale. Death is costly enough to matter without requiring a persistent world economy.

Alternative: return whenever you dare

Explore from a shelter, gather resources and decide when to bank them. This naturally creates frightening returns and strong “one more room” decisions. However, it also creates retreat exploits, farming incentives, persistent economy balancing and tension between banking safely and experimenting with a risky build.

Defer it. It could support the forest horror well, but is less direct for the requested Balatro-like complete-run arc. It would replace the recommended run architecture, not be an extra mechanic casually bolted onto it.

Alternative: a persistent Souls-like expedition

Open shortcuts, recover lost currency and maintain long-lived equipment across a connected forest/hospital world. This supports place familiarity and exploration mastery, but requires more interconnected level design, save states and progression content. It weakens the reason to rebuild from scratch every run.

Do not start here. It is closer to a full action RPG than the bounded roguelite proposed in this report.

5. The encounter loop and combat rules

Observe → position → prepare → evade → punish → reassess.
Example: identify the long sweep; land a heavy marking attack during the earlier recovery; lure the marked enemy toward water; dodge the sweep; spend the next opening on an amplified release. If the setup is unavailable, ordinary attacks remain useful.

Proposed first combat vocabulary
Action or rulePurposeConstraint
Normal movementCircle, retreat and choose an angle.No stamina cost. Do not make basic repositioning compete with defense.
Light strikeReliable low-commitment punish and a way to release prepared effects.Modest reach/damage; still has a readable active interval and recovery.
Heavy strikeHigher commitment, stronger stagger or a setup effect.Longer exposure. Its wind-up cannot be erased after the commitment point.
Directional dodgeAvoid an attack and reposition.Stamina cost, bounded invulnerability, recovery. No unlimited canceling out of active attacks.
Perfect dodgeOptional skill condition for some builds.Must evade an actual threatening attack during the designated short window, not merely roll near an enemy. Each hostile attack can award it once.
HealRecover health at a tactical cost.Limited charges; a committed action. Initial tuning should avoid making safe healing impossible or instant.
StaggerCreate an understandable punish opening.Visible buildup and recovery. Test boss loop prevention through recovery rules, not blanket immunity to all player effects.
Soft targetingReduce isometric aiming frustration.Assist within intended facing; never snap an attack behind the player. Compare this with optional lock-on during testing.

Only health, stamina and limited healing at first. No separate mana, hunger, temperature, sanity or crafting meters. An effect may have a short displayed charge, but that is not another resource economy. Normal movement permits stamina recovery; attack/dodge costs and the regeneration delay must be tuned together so the player can usually preserve one escape.

Responsiveness, commitments and fair failures
  • Define startup, commitment, active hit and recovery explicitly. A dodge can cancel only the designated early startup or late recovery regions—not every animation frame.
  • Buffer one next command near an action's end, rather than queueing a string of attacks the player cannot retract. The duration is a playtest parameter, not a sourced universal constant.
  • Display insufficient stamina clearly. Test whether the conventional “cannot dodge below cost” rule feels fair; a Tunic-like alternative is an experiment, not an automatic addition.
  • Enemy telegraphs, collision and facing must agree. Once an attack commits, do not let it invisibly rotate through the player to correct a miss.
  • Use visible poses as the primary attack information, with sound and ground cues as reinforcement. A sound-only tell is not accessible enough.
  • Death explanations distinguish poor timing, greed, hazard exposure, missed setup and unreadable feedback. A reported “I could not see it” death is a design issue to investigate, not a player-skill verdict.

Camera rule: begin with a fixed three-quarter camera over mostly planar arenas. Cut away roofs, fade occluding branches and keep hit-critical space in frame. Do not introduce jumping, stacked walkable floors or camera rotation until grounded combat is clear. Test actual weapon silhouettes at the intended display size, including any reduced-resolution treatment.

6. The build system: a small grammar, not a hundred cards

Equip one weapon and four alteration slots. Present alterations as readable reward cards, but make their identity material and physical once equipped. Four slots is a starting hypothesis: enough for a complete chain, few enough to remember during combat. A basic weapon must work with no alterations. The first prototype has four test effects; a later MVP might have twelve, not hundreds.

Primer
Create a target state or store potential: mark a body, load a heavy strike, accumulate a bounded stack.
Amplifier
Increase a prepared result under a stated condition: wet target, successful dodge, uninterrupted commitment or close-range exposure.
Converter
Change the result's use: focused damage becomes a line, burst becomes stagger, stored potential becomes protection. Defer most conversions until the core chain is readable.
Echo
Repeat a bounded part of the prepared result, potentially after a delay or at a marked location.

Begin with fixed resolution phases: existing target state → direct-hit bonuses → conditional multipliers → bounded repeat. Do not initially require free-form slot ordering in the middle of real-time combat. Balatro-like combinatorics do not require copying every Joker-order rule. Add player-controlled ordering only if a later test shows that it creates useful choices and can be explained in the preview.

A target already marked before the release may qualify; a mark created by that same release cannot retroactively qualify. This avoids same-event ambiguity.

Important: 5.4× burst damage is not 5.4× damage per second. Setup can cost an earlier attack and multiple enemy openings. Compare the entire encounter, not only the largest number. This example is not a proposed ceiling; later, rarer chains can be much stronger.

How to avoid infinite loops without removing the fun
  • Give each event a root attack identity, origin, target and tags such as direct, repeated and environmental. A card states which tags it accepts.
  • Resolve flat additions before multipliers. The echo snapshots the resolved release once; it does not spend the dodge charge again or reapply all multipliers.
  • In the first test, only direct hits create marks or start echoes. Echoes cannot echo themselves, mark new targets or grant another dodge charge.
  • This is a first-prototype simplification, not a permanent ban on proc interactions. Later effects can explicitly allow bounded propagation, a stated number of repeats or an acyclic chain of event types.
  • A single animation hit cannot damage the same body every frame. Define whether a multi-hit weapon spends a charge per swing or per hit before offering it.
  • Keep damage numbers free to grow. Prevent accidental recursion through rules and event validation rather than silently clamping a successful combo's damage.
  • A developer safety guard against runaway event processing is not a balance rule; if it fires, log and fix the offending interaction. Player-facing repeat limits must be stated on the card.

A good card text: “Your next hit after a perfect dodge deals ×2 damage.” The depth comes from choosing which hit receives it. A bad first card: multiple hidden status exceptions, random conversions and a long formula that must be read during an attack. Rules stay explicit even when strategic consequences are subtle [S5].

8. Rewards, economy and progression

  1. Give the first run a foundation. Offer a simple starter alteration and enough compatible early choices to form a partial engine. Do not make understanding the system depend on an exceptionally lucky four-card roll.
  2. After meaningful encounters, offer three visible choices. An extension, a pivot or an independently useful option is a design goal—not a promise that every generated offer will complete the player's build. Show condition tags and a comparison preview.
  3. Use one run currency initially. Salvage can buy healing, a card upgrade or a limited reroll at a workbench. Run-specific salvage disappears at the end; no premium-style currency layers.
  4. Let refitting be safe and cheap. Pause in reward screens and workbenches. Swaps occur outside combat, not while maintaining an active temporary buff. Start with a small reserve—such as two unequipped cards—if pivots otherwise become too punitive.
  5. Make upgrades qualitative where possible. Wider setup opportunity, another explicitly bounded repeat or altered coverage can compete with raw multiplication. Start with one upgrade tier, not an extensive rarity ladder.
  6. Make route previews honest. Tell the player encounter type, likely environmental conditions and reward category. Do not reveal every attack or exact item; do not conceal the information needed for a meaningful route decision.
  7. End with a useful recap. Show the largest chain, how often each alteration triggered, and a concrete death cause. “Rain conductor: equipped for four encounters, triggered zero times” suggests a learning opportunity or a bad design.

Meta-progression: unlock alternative weapons, starters, alterations and challenge routes; avoid permanent damage/health grinding initially. Discovering a new option should not make the offer pool progressively worse: provide opt-in unlock groups or curated pools later if dilution becomes visible. Prototype without meta-progression so base combat is not rescued by grinding.

Death: lose the current loadout and salvage, retain discovered rules and unlocks. No corpse run in the first version. Victory: complete the guardian and exit the route; optionally record the seed/build. Add difficulty variants only after the baseline has several viable tactics. Save-and-quit at safe nodes is valuable; offline operation does not require live services.

Do not “force experimentation” by withholding usable choices. Encourage it through visible synergies, safe comparison, varied route conditions and attractive alternatives. A player who wants a straightforward defensive build should still have a viable run.

10. Horror with low writing: anticipation, cost and release

Frictional's warning is especially relevant: constant aiming, loot evaluation and satisfying optimization consume the same attention that could support fear [S6]. We cannot simply add more fog and expect a math-heavy combat loop to behave like Amnesia. We also do not need to remove combat to satisfy your brief.

  1. Approach: the storm conceals whether a shape is occupied; a ripple or dragging sound establishes movement. The player can stop and watch before commitment.
  2. Commit: crossing a threshold or opening an optional cache reveals a visible threat and its escape/arena boundaries. No instant unavoidable hit upon interaction.
  3. Confront: the threat becomes legible. Combat emphasizes mastery rather than hiding rules.
  4. Break through: the build's release provides a brief reversal of power. Let the player enjoy it.
  5. Relief: reward selection and a genuinely safe rest. The next approach restores uncertainty about configuration or position—not whether the game has secretly changed the damage rules.

A small signature horror interaction

The occupied cache: an optional supply case stands beside a clearly visible inert shape. The player can inspect the reward category before opening it. Opening the case starts a short, readable awakening and an elite encounter; walking away leaves it alone. A reflection can reveal movement hidden by the direct viewing angle or a foreground prop; it should still show the same physical actor, not an independently animated false pose. Reuse one existing enemy rig with an authored awakening, not a new narrative character.

This supplies a physical question—“Is that reward worth waking it?”—and joins build greed to dread without dialogue. It is a proposed slice experiment, not a guaranteed scary mechanic. On repeat runs, the surprise will become knowledge; preserve the meaningful health/reward decision rather than pretending the same reveal stays shocking forever.

What to avoid, and where the writing work still exists
  • No sanity meter that randomly lies about active attack positions. No off-screen punishment justified as horror.
  • No global pursuer, noise economy or darkness simulation initially; those create AI, encounter and balance systems beyond the core loop.
  • No forced full-screen flash, constant camera shake or visual grain covering the weapon. Offer effect-intensity and accessibility controls.
  • Use compact environmental context: abandoned equipment, signs of recent movement, a broken barrier. This is still authored communication, but not a dialogue script or a lore archive.
  • Card text, tutorials, UI terminology and short objective labels still require clear writing. “Low narrative workload” is not “zero writing or zero sound/level-design work.”
  • If players consistently feel only excitement, do not label it frightening because the setting is gloomy. Test fear separately from satisfaction and be prepared to change pacing.

Functional enemy roles, not a cast or lore commitment. For the toy prototype, implement only the first role.

First guardian proposal: combine the sentinel's sweep with a clear forward attack and one ground-denial action. Give it longer, readable recoveries after its most dangerous commitments. A powerful chain may skip substantial health or end the fight quickly; that is acceptable if the setup was earned.

Do not solve overpowering builds with universal status immunity, invisible damage caps or a scripted one-hit-per-phase gate. First examine how often the setup succeeds, how much exposure it requires and whether the player can ignore all movement. If stagger locks the boss indefinitely, try a communicated recovery interval before another full stagger—not immunity to the rest of the build. Boss phase rules, if later added, must be explicit and should not erase a legitimate pending echo without explanation.

Encounter composition: begin with one enemy, then two complementary roles. Three separate wind-ups, reflection highlights, rain and four proc visuals can overwhelm a fixed camera. Difficulty should first come from combinations of understandable problems, not denser visual clutter or endlessly inflated health.

12. Procedural variation and artistic directions

Authored rooms, variable routes. First build arenas with reliable sightlines, wet/dry space, entry views and enemy placement rules. Later vary a small route graph, encounter combinations, reward offers and selected weather states. Seed those choices for repeatable tests. Do not begin with arbitrary procedural forest geometry or clinic layouts [S1].

All four recent art directions remain compatible. The mechanical grammar should work with plain debug materials. Once it does, use the selected construction language to make the same states recognizable; avoid changing rules merely to suit a concept image.

Concrete tests for stage A

  1. Fight the sentinel with no alterations. Observe whether players can identify startup, commitment and recovery, and can explain a death.
  2. Equip Needle seal alone. Check whether marking changes the attack choice without making ordinary damage useless.
  3. Add Rain conductor. Observe whether players deliberately reposition the enemy, rather than only notice a larger number.
  4. Add Counterweight and Standing echo. Ask players to predict the release, then watch whether they can actually perform it. A correct prediction with frequent failed execution suggests a timing/setup issue, not a tooltip issue.
  5. Repeat on dry ground and with a missed echo. The partial engine should remain viable; show why the total changed.
  6. Test terminal echo and one-charge rules mechanically: no self-repeat, no same-hit mark consumption, no multiple charges from one enemy attack.
  7. Only then add rain, selected reflections and the candidate style. Repeat the same tasks; visual treatment must not make them harder to read.
Recommended next implementation, only after your approval: stage A in Godot with plain or very simple styled materials. Do not start by constructing the entire forest or producing a dozen enemies.

14. What to measure, and when to redesign

Use a small number of observed sessions first; descriptive logs diagnose problems, but a few testers do not establish statistical balance. Later compare matched encounter seeds and players of different skill levels. The lesson from the Slay the Spire material is to combine data with feedback, not to choose a universal win-rate target from another genre [S3].

Redesign signals

  • Base combat is dull: stop content production and repair movement, enemy openings and hit response.
  • Players cannot explain the combo: simplify state transitions and improve the damage preview.
  • One family removes every positioning decision: improve tactical trade-offs and alternative opportunities; do not immediately forbid strong results.
  • Every reward feels like a small percentage upgrade: add a behavior-changing interaction, not more rarities.
  • Everyone ignores water: its rule may not create a real decision. Improve the interaction or leave water as presentation rather than pretending it matters.
  • The setting is gloomy but nobody feels tension: the horror hypothesis is failing. Adjust pacing and risky commitments, or honestly position the game as dark action.

Review decisions before implementation: approve or revise the short-run architecture; agree that temporary overpowering is desirable; choose whether stamina/dodge is conventional for the initial test; defer the final art direction until motion comparison. None of these choices is silently settled by this report.

The GDC links below were checked through their public overviews; I am not claiming to have watched the full talks. No external source contains this proposed game design, and no report example is a measured playtest result.

  1. [S1] Dead Cells. Sébastien Bénard, “Dead Cells: What the F*n!?” — GDC Vault (public session overview), and developer presentation transcript hosted by Ars Technica. Used for control fairness, small-detail iteration and handcrafted-room strategy. Not evidence for a particular dodge timing in this game.
  2. [S2] Balatro. LocalThunk interview — Rogueliker, 7 March 2024. Direct developer answers on interlocking systems, synergies, onboarding and Joker iteration. Published through an interview outlet.
  3. [S3] Slay the Spire. Anthony Giovannetti, “Slay the Spire: Metrics Driven Design and Balance” — GDC Vault. Public overview, not a full-video review. Supports measuring outcomes together with community feedback and game feel.
  4. [S4] MDA. Robin Hunicke, Marc LeBlanc and Robert Zubek, “MDA: A Formal Approach to Game Design and Game Research” (2004); AAAI paper entry. A conceptual framework, not a formula that guarantees fun.
  5. [S5] Lenticular design. Mark Rosewater, “Lenticular Design” — Wizards of the Coast, 31 March 2014. Used to distinguish readable rules from strategic depth and to limit on-board complexity. Its card-game context needs adaptation to real-time combat.
  6. [S6] Horror and attention. Thomas Grip / Frictional Games, “9 Years, 9 Lessons on Horror” (October 2019). Direct developer argument about attention, optimization, scares and narrative. Its priorities favor fear; this report intentionally keeps your combat/power goal rather than adopting those priorities wholesale.
  7. [S7] Darkwood. Official Steam description; official site. Establishes the exploration/preparation/refuge contrast and the original game's narrative scope. Marketing description, not an implementation study.
  8. [S8] Isometric combat. Andrew Shouldice interview — Game Rant, 27 February 2022. Developer testimony on Tunic's combat, stamina/dodge behavior and iterative design. This report does not propose its manual/language system.
  9. [S9] Small core before expansion. LocalThunk, “The Balatro Timeline”. First-person development history: chip × multiplier mechanics preceded many later features. A chronology, not a claim that this project should follow the same schedule.

Bottom line: borrow responsiveness and authored encounter quality from Dead Cells, interlocking conditional power from Balatro, iterative evidence from Slay the Spire, and preparation/anticipation from horror design. Validate the collision between those goals with one arena before committing to a full game.

Research synthesis and original proposals by Devin. All mechanics, numbers and scope estimates are unimplemented and unvalidated. Source links are references only; the document works offline.
